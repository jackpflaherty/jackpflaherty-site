The Tool That Showed Up on the Unit

Half the team is using it by Friday. The other half is waiting for someone to say it is safe. Decide, then watch for the ripple.

By Jack P. Flaherty. October 2026.

A new tool shows up on the unit. Sometimes it came through procurement. More often it came through a vendor update, a pilot in one department, or a nurse who found something that saves twenty minutes a shift. By Friday half the team is using it. The other half is waiting for someone to say it is safe.

Nobody owns the decision. So fear makes it. And fear’s decision is the worst one available: the tool is in use, unreviewed, by the people least able to see how it fails, while the people who could see it are waiting for permission nobody is giving.

Why health systems get this backwards

Health systems are better at controls than almost any organisation I have worked with. Medication reconciliation. The time-out before surgery. Two identifiers before a transfusion. Those controls exist because the organisation decided, once, that the risk of acting without them was higher than the cost of the check.

New technology rarely gets that decision. It gets a committee, a request for a policy, and a quiet workaround on the floor while the committee meets. I served as acting enterprise risk and audit leader for a $10B health system, reporting to the C-suite and the audit committee, and the pattern I saw there is the pattern I see everywhere: the tool is already in use by the time the risk conversation starts.

What to do the week it shows up

Treat the tool like a new hire. On the unit that means four things, and none of them needs a committee.

Give it a job. In one paragraph, what is the tool for, and what must it never do? “It drafts the handoff note” is a job. “It replaces the handoff conversation” is not.

Name its manager. One person on the unit owns how it is used and answers for it. The nurse manager is usually the right person, because she is the one the team already looks to for a yes or a no.

Set its probation. For a fixed period, someone checks a sample of what the tool produces against what a person would have done. Decide the sample and the period before Friday, not after the first near miss.

Review it. Decide, then watch for the ripple. What moved? Did the time it saved show up as time at the bedside, or as more tasks? What did people stop doing because the tool did it? What did the vendor change?

Decide, then watch

The ripple is where the real risk lives. A tool that is usually right changes what people check, and the time it is wrong lands on whichever patient is in the bed that day.

The teams that adopt new technology well are not the ones with the longest evaluation. They decide early, with conditions, and then they watch. That is not slower. It is the only version of fast that holds up when the regulator, the board or a family asks what happened.

Fear is not a control. A nurse manager with a job description, a sample and a review date is.
