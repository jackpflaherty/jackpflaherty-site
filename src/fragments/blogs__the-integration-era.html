The Clear Confidence Report

The Integration Era

Information is now a commodity. The value is in the people who connect it, and the decisions they own.

By Jack P. Flaherty. First published January 2026. Updated October 2026.

For decades professional services ran on one premise: the client had an information deficit and the firm charged by the hour to fill it. That premise is gone. A model can produce a risk analysis in seconds. So where does the value sit now?

It sits in integration.

The room with six languages

At Orion Health I was asked to prove that sharing healthcare data could cut cost and improve patient care. The team I built had clinicians, data scientists, programmers, cybersecurity specialists and regulatory leads. Six professional languages in one room. The project did not succeed because anyone had the most data. It succeeded because each discipline’s expertise was respected and their separate goals were pulled into one decision that someone owned.

Health systems and firms face that same room every time a new technology arrives. The clinical team wants it on the unit by Friday. Security wants ninety days. Compliance wants a policy. Finance wants the business case. Nobody is wrong, and nobody owns the call, so the tool drifts into use and fear makes the decision by default.

What clients are actually buying

Harvard Business Review’s research on the disruption of consulting put it plainly: clients no longer want opaque, time-based reports. They want transparent, measurable, integrated outcomes.1 The deliverable is not the analysis. It is the decision the analysis makes possible, and the person who stands behind it.

My father ran a wholesale printing company without a degree. He did not sell paper. He integrated himself into his clients’ businesses, understood what they were trying to do and solved for that. He operated on principles over profits, and it earned him a comfortable retirement and real respect. I swept his floors at ten and learned the job before I had a word for it.

Become the connective tissue

If your firm is handing clients disjointed analytics, you are already obsolete. The work now is to bridge the gap between the cybersecurity fear, the financial reality and the operational bottleneck, and to hand the client one decision they can make.

The method I use with boards: treat new technology like a new hire. Write its job description so the six languages agree on what it is for. Name its manager. Give it a probation period. Review it. That is integration with an owner.

Look at your deliverables from last quarter. Did you give your clients more data to decipher, or one decision they could act on with peace of mind?

The firms and health systems that thrive in this era will not be the ones with the smartest software. They will be the ones with the empathy to listen, the integrity to say what is true, and a leader willing to own the call.
