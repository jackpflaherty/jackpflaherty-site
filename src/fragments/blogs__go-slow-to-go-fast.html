Go Slow to Go Fast

What an audit chair should ask before the system goes live, and why asking it speeds the rollout up.

By Jack P. Flaherty. October 2026.

Every board has sat through the presentation. A new platform, an AI tool, a system that will touch patients or clients or the ledger. Management wants a decision. The slides are confident. The timeline is tight. And somewhere in the room a director is wondering whether the question in their head will sound like obstruction.

Ask it. The directors I have watched do this well are not the ones who slowed the organisation down. They are the ones who made the rollout faster, because they forced the one conversation that otherwise happens after go-live, in a crisis, with the regulator on the line.

Go slow to go fast is not caution. It is sequencing.

The two speeds

There are two ways to be slow about new technology. The first is to defer: ask for another analysis, another pilot, another quarter. That feels prudent, and it is the most expensive option there is, because the technology does not wait for the board. It arrives anyway, through a department, a vendor update or a contract nobody read closely. The board ends up ratifying what already happened.

The second is to be slow for one meeting and fast for the next two years: settle, before go-live, who owns this, what it is allowed to do, and when the board will see it again. That meeting costs an hour. It buys the organisation the right to move without stopping to re-argue the decision every time something goes wrong.

Four questions for the audit chair

I spent 15 years at the Big 4, where I served as a director, and I have reported to an audit committee as acting enterprise risk and audit leader for a $10B health system. These are the four questions that separate a decision from a drift.

Who is the manager? One named executive owns the tool’s output and answers to this committee for it. If the answer is a vendor, a project team or “IT”, the tool has no manager.

What is its job description? One page: what it is allowed to decide, what it may recommend, and what it must never touch. A board does not need to understand the model. It needs to understand the boundary.

What is the probation period? For how long, and on what sample, will a person check the tool’s work by hand before the organisation trusts it? Who signs off at the end?

When does this committee see it again? Not “we will keep you updated”. A date, and the three things management will bring: where it was wrong, what people changed to work around it, and what the vendor changed.

Treat new technology like a new hire. Boards already know how to oversee a senior hire. The same four instruments work for a system.

What this buys you

A board that asks these four questions once can say yes to the next ten decisions faster, because the shape of the answer is already agreed. Management knows what the committee will ask. The risk function knows what it has to produce. Nobody has to wait for someone to say it is safe, because safe has been defined.

Fear is not a control. A named manager, a one-page job description, a probation sample and a date on the committee calendar are. Decide, then watch for the ripple.
