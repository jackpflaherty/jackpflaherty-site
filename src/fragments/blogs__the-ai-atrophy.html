The Clear Confidence Report

The AI Atrophy

When the machine does the reps, your people lose the instinct that catches its mistakes.

By Jack P. Flaherty. First published February 2026. Updated October 2026.

My father ran a wholesale printing company. He never went to college. He could hear a sixteenth of an inch of misalignment in a press from a shift in its roar. Nobody taught him that. He earned it standing next to the machine.

I swept his floors at ten. In my twenties I traded derivatives at Morgan Stanley. Later I spent 15 years at the Big Four, where I served as a director. Different rooms, same lesson. The people who caught the problem early were the ones who had done the work by hand long enough to feel when something was off.

Accounting and consulting firms are now moving that work to AI. I am for it. The efficiency is real. But one question keeps coming back: when the system does the reps, where does the judgement come from?

What atrophy looks like

The danger is not a glaring error. Those get caught. The danger is a small contextual mistake in an output nobody checks, reviewed by a team that no longer has the instinct to doubt it.

The research has a name for this. When people rely on automated systems they stop monitoring and start trusting, and they miss the cases the system gets wrong. Parasuraman and Manzey laid it out in an integrative review of the research on automation complacency and bias.1

In a firm it shows up quietly. A senior manager signs off on a reconciliation she did not trace. An associate accepts a risk score he could not have built. The firm’s muscle memory, the thing clients pay for, thins out one unchecked output at a time.

Ask yourself: if the system went down tomorrow, could your second-year analysts rebuild the analysis from the raw data? If the answer is no, the tool is not assisting your people. It is replacing a skill you have not decided to give up.

Treat the tool like a new hire

Here is the method I use with boards and executive teams. Treat new technology like a new hire.

A new hire gets a job description. Write down what the tool is allowed to decide and what it is not.

A new hire gets a manager. One named person owns the tool’s output and answers for it.

A new hire gets a probation period. For the first ninety days, a person rebuilds a sample of the work by hand and compares.

A new hire gets a review. Decide, then watch for what moves: error rates, client questions, the things your people stopped noticing.

The firms that handle this well are not slower to adopt. They are faster, because they can say yes with conditions instead of waiting for someone else to prove it is safe. Fear is not a control. A probation period is.

Keep the reps

Embrace the analytics. Then build friction back into training on purpose. Make juniors audit the machine’s logic before they accept it. Make seniors explain a number without the dashboard. You are not slowing the firm down. You are keeping the instinct that tells you when the press is a sixteenth of an inch off.
