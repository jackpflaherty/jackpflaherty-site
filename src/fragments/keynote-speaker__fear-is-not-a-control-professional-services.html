Fear Is Not A Control

A keynote for partners in accounting, advisory and law firms

Making Technology Decisions Your Partners and Clients Will Stand Behind

A standing ovation is not the deliverable.

The deliverable is a room that leaves with three questions to ask before any technology decision, in the firm or at a client, and one first move for the next partner meeting.

Jack Flaherty is the trusted advisor who helps boards and executives make decisions with technology. He has spent 25 years in risk and advisory, 15 of them at the Big Four, where he served as a director.

The problem

Most keynotes are rented for the day. The insight leaves with the speaker.

01

Leaders are trained in everything except how to decide.

Firms invest in strategy, culture and technology. Almost none invest in the one skill that determines whether any of it works: making a confident, well-informed call under pressure. The gap shows up as stalled rollouts, partners working around the tool and teams waiting for permission nobody is giving.

02

The decision was already made. Nobody in the room knew it.

By the time most technology decisions reach the partner meeting, the outcome is settled. A tool is in use on an engagement, a vendor is signed, a client has already asked. The partners in the room are ratifying, not deciding. The most dangerous decisions are the ones a team made without noticing, and fear made most of them.

03

The signals were always there. Nobody was assigned to watch.

Every major failure Jack has sat close to had early warning signs. The organizations that came through were not smarter. Someone had the job of reading what the others ignored. Decide, then watch for what moves.

The teams that adopt new technology well are not the ones with the best plan. They are the ones who gave the decision an owner.

Why Jack

He has sat in the rooms where technology decisions get made, defended and paid for.

Across 25 years in risk and advisory, 15 of them at the Big Four, Jack noticed one pattern: the leaders who struggled most were not the least talented. They were the least equipped to make a call and stand behind it under pressure. Most of them were waiting for a control that did not exist, and fear filled the gap.

That observation became a method. The method became one keynote, built for two rooms, and the same questions he asks a board when a new system is about to go live.

The audience leaves with

Four things that outlast the applause.

01

A method, not a feeling.

Treat new technology like a new hire. A job description, a manager, a probation period, a review. Every partner in the room can apply it to the next tool, vendor or system that lands on an engagement.

02

A name for what has been slowing them down.

Nobody owns the decision, so fear makes it. Once a room can say that out loud, it can assign the decision. This keynote gives them the language.

03

A contrarian view on risk they will argue about on the way home.

Governance has a reputation for slowing things down, and sometimes it earns it. Fear is slower, and nobody can test it. The risk person in the room is the one saying go, on conditions.

04

Something they walk out ready to teach.

The three questions are short enough to repeat without a slide. A partner who attended should be able to walk a senior manager through them the next morning.
