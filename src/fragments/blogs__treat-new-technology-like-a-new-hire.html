Treat New Technology Like a New Hire

Nobody would hire a person without a job description, a manager, a probation period and a review. Most organisations adopt technology without any of them.

By Jack P. Flaherty. October 2026.

Think about the last person your organisation hired into a senior role. Someone wrote a job description. Someone was named as the manager. There was a probation period, and a review at the end of it, and everyone understood that the hire could be let go if it did not work out.

Now think about the last technology your organisation adopted. Who wrote its job description? Who is its manager? When is its review?

In most organisations the answers are nobody, nobody and never. The tool arrived through a contract, a pilot or a free trial someone forgot to cancel, and it has been doing whatever it wants ever since. That is not a technology problem. It is a management problem, and managers already know how to solve it.

Step one: the job description

Write down, in one page, what the tool is for. What decisions it is allowed to make on its own. What decisions it is allowed to recommend. What it must never touch. If the vendor cannot help you fill in that page, that tells you something.

The job description is where most AI arguments get settled before they start. “Can the model draft the client letter?” is a job description question. So is “can it send it?” A yes to the first and a no to the second is a perfectly good answer, and it fits on one line.

Step two: the manager

One named person owns the tool’s output and answers for it. Not the vendor. Not IT. Not a steering committee. A person whose name the board can say.

This is the step organisations skip, because naming a manager means naming who gets the call when the tool is wrong. That is exactly why it matters. A tool with no manager is a tool nobody is watching.

Step three: the probation period

For a fixed period, the organisation does the work twice: the tool does it, and a person checks a sample by hand. Set the sample size and the period before the tool goes live, not after the first complaint.

The probation period is what lets a risk-minded leader say yes to something new. You are not approving the tool forever. You are approving ninety days of it, with a review at the end.

Step four: the review

Decide, then watch for the ripple. At the end of probation, sit down and look at three things: where the tool was wrong, what the people around it changed to work with it, and what the vendor changed without telling you. Then decide again: keep it, expand it, restrict it or let it go.

Why this works

The method borrows the one process every organisation already runs well. Nobody needs training to understand it. A nurse manager, an audit partner and an audit committee chair can all repeat it on the drive home, and all three can apply it to the next tool that lands on their desk.

Fear says wait until it is safe. The new-hire method says it is safe once it has a job, a manager, a probation period and a review. That is the difference between a control and a feeling.
